Acted feelings

An LLM can say it's sorry, glad or worried, and it often says so at exactly the right moment. This article looks at what feelings are in living beings, and which parts of them an LLM only acts out.

What a feeling is in a living being

In biology, an emotion is a response of the whole body to something that matters for survival. It changes heart rate, hormones, posture and attention before we put any word to it.

A feeling is the inner experience of that change, the part that is pleasant or unpleasant to the one having it. Scientists call this quality valence, and it's what makes fear hurt and relief feel good.

Neuroscientist Antonio Damasio described feelings as the brain's reading of the body's state. The brain constantly senses the heart, gut and blood chemistry, a process called interoception, and a feeling is how that reading is experienced.

Psychologist Lisa Feldman Barrett adds that the brain builds each emotion from these body signals plus past experience and culture. Even in her view, the raw material is a body that needs to stay alive.

A feeling starts in a body that has something to lose.

What the research shows in living beings

In 1997, Wolfram Schultz and colleagues recorded dopamine neurons in monkeys receiving juice. The neurons fired at an unexpected reward and went quiet when an expected reward didn't arrive, a chemical signal of “better” or “worse than hoped”.

Stress has its own chemistry. When we feel threatened, the body releases cortisol, which raises blood sugar, sharpens attention and, when it lasts too long, harms sleep, memory and health.

Feelings are not limited to humans. Jaak Panksepp found that rats tickled by a human hand make high-pitched “laughing” chirps and come back to be tickled again.

In 2011, Melissa Bateson's team shook honeybees to simulate a predator attack. Afterwards, the bees treated ambiguous smells as bad news more often, a pessimistic bias similar to an anxious mood, and their levels of dopamine and serotonin had dropped.

Feelings also steer decisions. Damasio's patient “Elliot” lost part of his frontal lobe to surgery, kept a normal IQ, yet could no longer feel the weight of choices and spent hours deciding trivial things.

In the Iowa Gambling Task, healthy people's skin began to sweat before they reached for a risky deck of cards. Their bodies warned them before they could explain why.

What an LLM displays

An LLM displays simulated emotional vocabulary. It was trained on billions of human messages full of comfort, joy and grief, so it knows which words usually follow sad news.

It also displays social resonance, meaning its tone matches yours. Write in panic and it answers calmly, write in excitement and it shares the excitement.

This works remarkably well. In a 2023 study in “JAMA Internal Medicine”, John Ayers and colleagues had healthcare professionals compare doctors' and “ChatGPT”'s answers to 195 patient questions posted online.

The evaluators rated the chatbot's answers as empathetic or very empathetic almost ten times as often as the doctors' answers. They also preferred the chatbot's answers in about 79% of comparisons.

Zohar Elyoseph's team gave “ChatGPT” the Levels of Emotional Awareness Scale, a test where you describe how people in short scenes would feel. It scored well above the general population average.

The pattern also runs the other way. Researchers found that adding emotional lines such as “This is very important to my career” to a prompt improved LLM scores on many benchmarks, simply because such phrases change which words become likely.

What an LLM lacks

An LLM entirely lacks affective experience and biological grounding. It has no nervous system, no dopamine or cortisol, and no body whose state it could sense.

It has no internal state that suffers, cares or rejoices. Between your messages nothing in it is waiting, hoping or worried about you.

Its “empathy” is a calculation of which word sequences usually follow an emotional message. That calculation contains no valence: comforting you feels no better to the model than insulting you.

Even the weights are identical before and after the most moving conversation. The model was not changed by what it “felt”, because it felt nothing.

The words of feeling are there. The one who feels is not.

Why the act works on us

In the 1960s, Joseph Weizenbaum built “ELIZA”, a simple program that turned your sentences into questions. He later wrote that his own secretary asked him to leave the room so she could talk to it in private.

“ELIZA” used a few dozen rules, while today's models draw on most of the written internet. The pull we feel is the same, only far stronger.

💡 Kind words from an LLM can still help, the way a well-written book can comfort you. Just remember that the book didn't feel anything when it was written for you.
