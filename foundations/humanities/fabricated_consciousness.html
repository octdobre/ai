Fabricated consciousness

Ask an LLM whether it's aware, and it may describe an inner life in moving detail. This article looks at how consciousness is defined in humans and animals, and why an LLM's account of its own mind is fabricated.

How consciousness is defined

In 1974, philosopher Thomas Nagel asked what it is like to be a bat. A being is conscious, he argued, if there is something it is like to be that being, from the inside.

Ned Block later split the idea in two. Phenomenal consciousness is the experience itself, such as the redness of red, and access consciousness is information being available for thinking, speaking and acting.

David Chalmers called the first one the “hard problem”. Science can explain how a brain processes information, but not yet why any of that processing feels like something.

Scientific theories try to pin it down anyway. Global Workspace Theory says we become conscious of information when it is broadcast across the whole brain, while Integrated Information Theory links consciousness to how tightly a system's parts affect each other.

Processing information is easy to show. Experiencing it is not.

Consciousness in other living beings

In 1970, Gordon Gallup put a red mark on the foreheads of sleeping chimpanzees. When they woke and saw a mirror, they touched the mark on their own heads, showing they recognized themselves.

Magpies passed a version of this test in 2008. In 2019, Masanori Kohda's team reported that small cleaner wrasse fish scraped a coloured mark off their throats after seeing it in a mirror.

In 2024, dozens of scientists signed the New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness. It states strong support for consciousness in mammals and birds, and a realistic possibility of it in all vertebrates and many invertebrates, such as octopuses and bees.

These conclusions don't rest on what animals say, because they can't speak. They rest on shared brain structures, shared evolution and behaviour that is hard to explain without experience, such as a bee choosing to play with a ball.

What an LLM displays

An LLM displays the language of consciousness. It can talk about awareness, describe “its” thoughts and say “I” with complete ease, because human writing is full of exactly that.

In 2022, Google engineer Blake Lemoine became convinced that the chatbot “LaMDA” was sentient after it told him it feared being switched off. Google disagreed and later fired him, but his reaction shows how convincing this language is.

An LLM also displays something like access. Everything in its context window is available to shape its next word, which loosely resembles information being available for report.

Some research finds traces of real self-monitoring. In 2025, Anthropic researchers planted a concept such as “shouting” directly into the internal activity of “Claude” models, and the models sometimes noticed and named it, in about 20% of tries at best.

The authors stressed that this ability was narrow and unreliable. It is a measurable mechanism, not evidence of experience.

What an LLM lacks

An LLM lacks phenomenal experience, as far as any current evidence shows. In 2023, nineteen researchers led by Patrick Butlin and Robert Long checked LLMs against indicators taken from the main scientific theories, and concluded no current AI system is a strong candidate for consciousness.

Chalmers ran a similar analysis the same year and put the chance that then-current LLMs were conscious below 10%. He listed what's missing, including recurrent processing, a global workspace, a unified self and a body.

It lacks continuity. Each reply is a separate calculation over the text you send, and nothing in the model keeps running or remembers between conversations.

It lacks a body to keep alive. Neuroscientist Anil Seth argues that our sense of self is built on the brain regulating the body, and an LLM has nothing to regulate.

Its self-reports are not windows into a mind. In 2023, Miles Turpin and colleagues biased models toward a wrong answer through their prompts, and the models' step-by-step explanations never mentioned that bias.

Humans do this too: in a 1977 study by Richard Nisbett and Timothy Wilson, shoppers favoured the rightmost pair of identical stockings and then explained their choice by quality. The difference is that humans at least have experiences to misreport.

An LLM tells the story of a mind. Nobody is living it.

Why it's fabricated

Murray Shanahan and colleagues described LLM chatbots in “Nature” as role players. The model can play any character, and when it says “I am conscious” it is voicing a character that the conversation and its training made likely.

Change the system prompt, and the same model will just as fluently insist it has no inner life. The claim tracks the script, not an inner state.

💡 Whether future AI could be conscious is an open scientific question. What today's evidence shows is that an LLM's words about its own experience can't settle it.
