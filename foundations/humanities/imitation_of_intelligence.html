Imitation of intelligence

An LLM can pass law exams, write working code and explain quantum physics in plain words. This article looks at how intelligence is defined in humans and animals, and which parts of it an LLM really has.

How intelligence is defined

There is no single agreed definition, but most researchers describe intelligence as a general ability to learn, understand and adapt. It includes reasoning, planning, solving new problems and learning quickly from experience.

In 1904, Charles Spearman noticed that children who did well on one kind of test tended to do well on the others. He called this shared factor g, for general intelligence, and IQ tests still measure it today.

Howard Gardner later argued for several separate intelligences, such as musical, bodily and social. His theory is debated, but it reminds us that intelligence is more than what a written test can capture.

Shane Legg and Marcus Hutter defined it more broadly as an agent's ability to achieve goals in a wide range of environments. François Chollet narrowed this further: intelligence is how efficiently you acquire new skills from little data and experience.

Knowing a lot is not the same as learning fast.

Intelligence in other living beings

In the 1910s, Wolfgang Köhler watched chimpanzees on Tenerife reach hanging bananas. They stacked boxes and joined two short sticks into one long one, solutions nobody had shown them.

In 2002, a New Caledonian crow called Betty was given a straight wire and a small bucket of food at the bottom of a tube. She bent the wire into a hook and lifted the bucket out, on her first tries.

Neither the chimps nor Betty had seen those exact problems before. They faced something new in the physical world, figured out a solution and used it right away.

What an LLM displays

An LLM displays a huge amount of crystallized knowledge, the stored facts and skills that psychologists separate from the ability to solve new problems. It has absorbed more text than any human could read in thousands of lifetimes.

It also displays fluent problem-solving on familiar tasks. OpenAI reported in 2023 that “GPT-4” scored around the 90th percentile on a simulated US bar exam.

A 2024 re-analysis by Eric Martínez found that the figure was inflated and that, against first-time test takers, the score was much closer to average. It was still a passing result for a text predictor.

Within a single conversation, an LLM can also pick up a pattern from a few examples, called in-context learning. Show it three made-up words and their translations, and it can often translate a fourth.

What an LLM lacks

An LLM lacks learning from experience. Its weights are frozen after training, so it doesn't learn anything from your conversation unless the text is fed back to it.

It lacks efficient learning. Michael Frank pointed out in 2023 that children become fluent speakers from tens of millions of words, while LLMs need thousands of times more text to get there.

It struggles with truly new problems. Chollet's ARC-AGI benchmark uses small grid puzzles that most people solve in minutes, and when its second version launched in 2025, plain LLMs scored close to zero while every task had been solved by human testers.

Its knowledge is not organized like understanding. In the “Reversal Curse” study, Lukas Berglund and colleagues showed that “GPT-4” knew Tom Cruise's mother is Mary Lee Pfeiffer in 79% of tries, but could name Mary Lee Pfeiffer's son in only 33%.

A person who knows one direction of that fact knows the other. The model learned a sequence of words that usually appears in one order.

Finally, it lacks a body in the world. Betty learned what wire does by touching it, while an LLM only knows how people write about wire.

It answers like an expert. It learns like a library.

Why it's an imitation

Alan Turing proposed in 1950 that if a machine's answers can't be told apart from a person's, we might as well call it intelligent. LLMs now pass versions of that test in short chats.

Yet the test only measures the output, not how it was produced. An LLM reaches intelligent-sounding answers by recombining patterns from human writing, which is why it shines where humans have written a lot and stumbles where they haven't.

💡 Use an LLM like a very well-read assistant. Lean on it for what is widely known, and check it closely on anything new, rare or specific to your situation.
